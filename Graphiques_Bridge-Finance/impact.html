Chapter seven

Impact

Success is not money distributed. It is people who recovered.

Bridge measures outcomes rather than activity. A program that moved a lot of money and left everyone in the same position has failed, and the measurement framework should say so.

Primary indicators

Six things worth counting.

Indicator one

Emergency resolution rate

The share of eligible emergencies that were actually resolved. The first question, and the easiest to measure honestly.

Indicator two

Recovery completion

The share of participants who completed their recovery pathway rather than stalling partway through it.

Indicator three

Savings rebuilding

The share who rebuilt emergency savings during or after recovery. This is the difference between relief and resilience.

Indicator four

Repeat emergency dependence

How often participants need further support. Watched as a warning signal. A rise triggers review rather than a bigger limit.

Indicator five

High-cost credit avoidance

Where it can be measured ethically and legally, whether participants avoided expensive emergency borrowing they would otherwise have used.

Indicator six

Resilience improvement

Change in the conceptual Resilience Profile between the shock and the end of the pathway.

Two of these are deliberately hard. High-cost credit avoidance requires a counterfactual, which means either self-report or consented data access, and both have limits worth stating rather than hiding. Resilience improvement uses a conceptual framework rather than a validated instrument, so it would need research before it could carry weight.

How it would be measured

Measurement that could survive being checked.

A concept project can state a measurement plan honestly even though it has no data. That is what this section is.

  • Baseline the Resilience Profile at intake and read it again at the end of the pathway, so change is measured against the same instrument
  • Track recovery completion by cohort and by sponsoring organisation, because a program that works at one employer may not transfer
  • Separate people who did not need support from people who were declined, and report both
  • Report repeat support rates prominently rather than quietly, since a product like this can fail by succeeding commercially
  • Collect high-cost credit avoidance through consented self-report at first, and state its limits every time it is published
  • Publish the definitions alongside the numbers, so an outside reader can tell what was counted

Bridge is a concept designed and submitted for the Graphiques Innovation Challenge. It is a proposal, not a product: it is not a live financial service, it does not lend money, it has no users, partners or revenue, and nothing here is financial advice. The Bridge Resilience Profile is a conceptual product framework for financial resilience planning. It is not a credit score and does not represent a lending decision. Every scenario, dashboard and pilot figure on this site is clearly labelled as illustrative. External statistics carry a named source, a year and a link. Any real implementation would require appropriate financial, legal, regulatory, compliance, consumer protection, privacy and risk review.

30 to 45 seconds · The intervention

Most financial products help people transact. Bridge is designed to help people recover.

Traditional credit asks whether a person can repay. Bridge adds a second question: what would help this person recover? That question changes the product from a loan into a recovery plan with support attached.

60 to 75 seconds · The business

Organisations fund resilience. Bridge runs it.

Bridge is business to business to consumer. Employers, universities, nonprofits and financial institutions establish a Resilience Fund and pay for the platform that runs it. Revenue comes from institutional contracts, not from interest charged to people in difficulty.
