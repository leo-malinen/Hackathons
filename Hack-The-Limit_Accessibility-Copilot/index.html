The difference

This is not an overlay.

People flinch at the words accessibility widget, and the flinch is earned. The National Federation of the Blind reports that overlays often get in the way of a screen reader. In one study, screen reader users walked past accessibility menus while working and not one of them opened one. So here is the difference, stated plainly, before anything else.

An overlay

The website's owner installs it, once, for every visitor at the same time.

Accessibility Copilot

You install it. It travels with you and works the same way on every site you visit.

Who it speaks for

It guesses what a visitor needs and announces itself to their screen reader whether they asked or not.

Who it speaks for

It does what you set and nothing else. It never guesses a diagnosis, an age, or a health condition about you.

When it gets it wrong

You are stuck with it, or you leave the site.

When it gets it wrong

Every change is listed in plain words with a stet beside it. One press puts one back. One press puts everything back.

Sources for the two claims above: National Federation of the Blind resolutions and a write-up of the screen reader study.

The honest half

And the things it cannot fix.

Every tool in this corner of the web tells you what it repairs. This one also tells you what it cannot, on the page you are standing on, in plain words. Printers have a name for that. When a proofreader finds something only the writer can put right, they raise an author query.

It will not touch these

Fixing any of them means guessing at what somebody meant, and a wrong guess is worse than the problem.

  • A picture with no description. Only the person who put it there knows what it shows.
  • A button with no name. The Copilot could guess from what sits nearby. On a button that spends money, it does not.
  • A link that reads "read more". Rewriting where a link says it goes can send you somewhere you did not choose.
  • A heading level skipped. Renumbering headings changes what a page claims about itself.
  • A field with no label. It will not name a box you are about to type into and might name wrongly.

It can cover these

It can make them bearable for you. The page is still broken underneath, for everyone who arrives without it.

  • Text under the contrast floor. High Contrast lifts it in a moment and fixes it for nobody else.
  • No main region to skip to. The Copilot adds a skip link pointed at its best guess.
  • Controls too small to hit. It can pad what you press without moving the layout around it.
  • Media that starts itself. Low Motion stops it here and nowhere else.

We ran it on all six pages of this site. It comes back clean. That is the least anyone is owed rather than something to be proud of, and the check only knows how to look for a dozen things. Open it on the demo page and it finds nine, because that sample page is built the way real council sites are built.

Who it is for

Six people, six settings, one page.

Profiles are named after what they do, never after a person or a condition. Nobody has to say what is wrong with them to read a website.

Reading long things

“Help me read a long article without losing my place.”

Comfortable type, a line that does not run on forever, a ruler to hold the line, and less to look at around it.

Working without a mouse

“Let me finish a task without reaching for a mouse.”

A focus ring you can find at a glance, a skip link that is really there, and the page's regions on show.

Wanting plainer words

“Help me understand this without changing what it means.”

Shorter sentences, a summary of what a page is asking you to do, and the original text kept beside the rewrite.

Seeing low contrast

“Make the important things easier to tell apart.”

Contrast pushed to its strongest, bigger type, and spacing that still holds up when the page is magnified.

Sensitive to movement

“Stop the page moving when I did not ask it to.”

No auto-play, no parallax, no drifting background, and no banner sliding past the corner of your eye.

Setting it up for someone else

“Set up one steady way of reading for someone I support.”

Build a profile once, export it as a small file, and hand it over. Nothing about the person goes in it.

Fenced off

Some things it will not touch.

Rewriting is the risky part, so the risky places are fenced off. In these areas the Copilot does the visual and keyboard work only. It will not reword any of it unless you ask for that passage by name, and when you do, it shows the original next to the rewrite.

  • Payment fields
  • Passwords and sign-in
  • Legal consent
  • Medication instructions
  • Emergency information
  • Anything you have typed

Straight answers

Three questions people actually ask.

Is this the same as those accessibility widgets on other sites?

No, and the difference is who it works for. A widget is bolted onto one site by that site's owner and speaks for every visitor at once. This is yours. It travels with you, it only does what you set, and when you switch it off it is off.

Does it make a website meet accessibility standards?

No. It helps a person read a page. It does not repair the page and it does not make anyone compliant with anything. We hold our own work to WCAG 2.2 as a quality target and we claim nothing at all about the sites you visit.

Where do my settings go?

Into your own browser and nowhere else. No account is needed, no page text is collected for training, and the delete button really deletes. If device sync ever arrives it will be something you switch on, never something already on.
